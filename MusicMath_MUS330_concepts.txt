High-level read
---------------
Music_Math.ipynb is a broad computational music-theory scratchpad. It explores harmony, pitch-class sets, tuning, MIDI generation, polyrhythms, and algorithmic etudes. It is less like lecture notes and more like a tool bench: short functions, numerical experiments, and MIDI-writing routines.

MUS 330.xlsx is much more focused. It is a spreadsheet for rhythmic measurement, especially irrational or uneven-feeling rhythm made practical through approximations, proportions, tempo relationships, and empirical timing measurements. The workbook seems to belong to Tymoczko's Advanced Concepts in Rhythm version of MUS 330.


Music_Math.ipynb concepts
-------------------------
1. Computational representation of music

The notebook uses libraries such as mingus, numpy, matplotlib/plotly, and MIDIUtil. The basic idea is to represent musical objects as data:
- notes as names or pitch classes;
- chords/scales as generated collections;
- pitch sets as 12-bit integers;
- rhythms and fingerings as strings or arrays;
- generated material as MIDI.

This turns theory into something searchable and generative rather than only notated by hand.


2. Pentatonic substitutions and dominant-color material

The "Pentatonics" section prints collections associated with dominant roots. It derives diminished-seventh collections, b6 relationships, and tritone/#4-type colors. Musically this is about improvisational vocabulary: given a dominant harmony, find pentatonic or symmetric note collections that produce altered dominant colors.

This fits Mahanthappa's world: modern jazz improvisation often treats scales and pentatonics as movable cells rather than fixed key signatures. The code is a way of generating those cells mechanically for practice.


3. Fourths / quartal-ish cells

The "Fourths" section generates random three-note cells from diatonic or dominant contexts. In the major case it chooses scale-degree patterns; in the dominant case it uses pitch-class templates such as [0, 1, 7] transposed around the root.

The likely concept is improvising with small, repeatable intervallic cells rather than full scales. This is a common way to create modern jazz lines: small shapes are moved through chord or scale spaces.


4. Polyrhythm random walks

The PolyRhythm class stores a vector such as [3, 4, 5] and randomly nudges one component at a time while keeping all values inside bounds. The output is a sequence of neighboring polyrhythmic ratios.

Conceptually, this treats polyrhythm as a point in a small integer space. Moving from [3, 4, 5] to [4, 4, 5] or [4, 3, 5] is like voice-leading, but for rhythmic ratios rather than pitches.


5. Symmetry, modes, and completion of pitch sets

The "Symmetry" code takes a pitch set and asks what other notes are implied by intervallic symmetry. For example, it examines relationships between notes, computes complementary intervals, and appends enharmonic results.

The musical concept is "what collection is implied by the internal interval structure of this fragment?" That is useful for creating nonstandard modes from small motives or chords.


6. Nonatonic collections and augmented-root systems

The "Nonatonic" section uses the augmented triad roots of a key and randomly chooses major/minor/sus2 chord qualities. This points toward 9-note scalar spaces made from combining three triads.

Conceptually: take a symmetric root skeleton, such as an augmented triad, and place triadic colors on it to create rich non-diatonic harmonic fields.


7. Pitch-class set enumeration

The PitchSetSet class enumerates 12-tone pitch-class sets up to transposition. It found 352 transposition classes, grouped by set size.

It represents each pitch-class set as a binary number. Example: a scale becomes a 12-bit on/off pattern. This makes it easy to:
- rotate/transposition-normalize sets;
- group by cardinality;
- convert sets to note names;
- score how "circle-of-fifths-like" a set is.


8. Circle-of-fifths scoring

The CoFScore method ranks pitch sets by how well they align with the circle of fifths. The notebook output ranks the diatonic collection highly and sorts diatonic modes by a fifth-based criterion. It prints an ordering such as Lydian, major/Ionian, Mixolydian, Dorian, Aeolian, Phrygian, Locrian under one scoring setup.

The idea: instead of saying "major scale is important" axiomatically, create a mathematical score for collections that are compact or coherent on the circle of fifths.


9. Pitch-set containment and pitch-set superpermutations

The notebook builds a containment dictionary: for each pitch set, which smaller sets appear inside it under cyclic rotation. It then searches for a small list or string whose substrings/segments cover many pitch sets.

This is analogous to a superpermutation or shortest common superstring problem:
- cover every target pattern;
- avoid redundant material;
- generate one compact sequence that contains many musical objects.

The notebook writes this as MIDI, including a compressed version where rests/zeroes are removed.

Conceptually, this is "practice or compose one sequence that contains all the pitch-set cases I care about."


10. Quarter-tone key signatures and EDO combinatorics

The notebook compares counts for 12-tone and 24-tone "key signatures":
- in 12-TET, circle-of-fifths key signatures are a tiny subset of all possible accidental assignments;
- in 24-TET, the number of possible non-circle-of-fifths accidental systems explodes.

It also notes slendro and pelog as octave divisions approximated by 2^(1/5) and 2^(1/7).

The concept is that notation systems are combinatorial filters: a conventional key signature is a highly constrained path through an enormous space of possible pitch spellings.


11. Superpermutation etudes for fingers

The "Etudes" section generates sequences that contain every possible finger pattern of a certain length. It includes:
- precomputed superpermutations for 1-5 symbols;
- piano and violin MIDI writers;
- replacement-lag constraints, such as "do not reuse a finger within the last N positions."

This directly relates to a Guitar-Hero-like or technique-drill etude generator. The goal is an efficient exercise that covers all small fingering patterns without wasting time.

The replacement-lag version is especially practical: it can forbid immediate repetition, making the generated etude closer to a real technical challenge.


12. Equal divisions of the octave and harmonic-series error

The "EDO tests with Harmonic Series" section measures how well EDO systems approximate harmonic ratios such as 3, 5, 7, and 9. It computes cents error between the harmonic ratio and the nearest EDO note.

The concept: tuning systems can be compared numerically by how well they approximate chosen just-intonation targets. Different EDOs become better or worse depending on which harmonics are weighted.


13. Ionization energies as musical pitches

The ionization-energy section reads NIST-like ionization-energy data, converts electronvolts to frequency using Planck's constant, maps those frequencies into pitch classes/octaves, and finds a best-fit log-frequency shift.

Conceptually this asks: "If atomic ionization energies were treated as frequencies, what keyboard/tuning would best represent them?"

This connects physical data to musical pitch space, and it explains why some of the site's periodic-table/music experiments exist.


14. Inferring diatonic context from a stream of notes

The inferDiatonic function receives pitch classes and maintains the set of possible major keys compatible with the notes seen so far. When the possible key set collapses to one key, it records an inferred span; when it becomes empty, it resets.

The concept is an online key-inference algorithm: as notes arrive, intersect the possible tonal contexts until a key is implied or contradicted.

1. Approximate Polyrhythms

This sheet studies how to approximate hard polyrhythms with playable fractional subdivisions.

The first blocks compare:
- ideal 5-over-7 spacing;
- ideal 7-over-5 spacing;
- approximate fractional placements;
- decimal versions;
- error as percent of the measure;
- error as percent of a quarter note.

Example idea: if an event "should" occur at 1/5 of the measure, maybe it is approximated by 4/21. The sheet then asks how far that is from the mathematically even point.

This is a very practical performer/composer question:
- exact tuplets can be hard to read or feel;
- approximate placements may be close enough;
- the sheet quantifies the cost of the approximation.


2. Error measured at multiple musical scales

The same timing error is measured both as:
- percent of an entire measure;
- percent of a beat/quarter-note unit.

That second framing matters more for performance. A 1% measure error can feel different depending on meter and tempo. The sheet asks: "How wrong is this in beat-sized units?"


3. Metric modulation while preserving measure duration

Rows around "Starting # of 8th notes/measure" model a sequence:
- 16 eighth notes at 192 bpm;
- 13 at 156 bpm;
- 10 at 120 bpm;
- 7 at 84 bpm;
- 4 at 48 bpm.

The computed measure duration stays at 2.5 seconds while the internal subdivision count and tempo change. Long/short ratios move from 1.2 to 2.0.

Conceptually this is rhythmic zooming:
- keep the outer container stable;
- change the internal grid;
- preserve continuity while altering perceived subdivision and ratio.


4. Tempo and millisecond conversion

The sheet converts BPM to milliseconds per quarter note and per sixteenth note. For example, 192 bpm gives 312.5 ms per quarter and 78.125 ms per sixteenth.

This is the bridge from notation to DAW/audio timing: if you are programming or analyzing rhythm, you often need durations in milliseconds, not only notation.


5. Ratio chains and proportional tempo ladders

Rows with values such as 81 -> 108 -> 144 -> 192 and 135 -> 225 -> 180 -> 144 use ratios like 4/3 and 5/3 or 4/5.

This treats tempo as a proportional network. The musical concept is metric modulation: one subdivision in the old tempo becomes a different subdivision in the new tempo.


6. Unequal beat divisions

The "Division" block compares 3+2 against 7+4:
- 3+2 gives 60% / 40%;
- 7+4 gives about 63.64% / 36.36%;
- the difference is about 3.64 percentage points.

This is about "lopsided" or aksak-like rhythm: patterns with unequal long/short beat groups can be compared by their proportional shapes.


7. Festejo sheet

Festejo is an Afro-Peruvian rhythm/dance tradition. The sheet measures many examples or repetitions as three durations labeled Long, Short, and Med.

Key computed values:

This suggests the sheet is trying to learn the real timing "feel" of a groove, not just its nominal notation. The long beat is not merely "twice" the short beat; it is about 1.62 times the short beat in this data.

How the two files relate
------------------------
Both files are about turning musical intuition into measurable objects.

Music_Math.ipynb does this mostly for pitch, tuning, sets, and generated etudes:
- scales become bitsets;
- finger patterns become strings;
- tuning systems become cents-error functions;
- atomic data becomes keyboard data.

MUS 330.xlsx does this mostly for rhythm:
- tuplets become fractions;
- grooves become measured duration ratios;
- tempo modulations become proportional transformations;
- timing feels become average proportions and deviations.

Together they point toward a strong personal theme: music theory as computational design. The point is not just to analyze music after the fact, but to make tools that generate practice material, find compact exercises, compare tuning systems, and quantify rhythmic feel.
